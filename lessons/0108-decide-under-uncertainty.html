Lesson 0108 · The Engineer's Prompt

Decide Under Uncertainty

You are about to lock a backend language, a graph schema, and a submission format with no way to test them first. Some of those you can change tomorrow for free; some you cannot. The rubric tells you which is which — and the AI should give you options, not a verdict.

By the end you can classify any Track 02 decision as mandatory, reversible, or expensive-to-reverse and act accordingly, evidenced by a three-line decision record for the Node-vs-Python fork plus the prompt you used to get options instead of an answer.

Why this matters

Every remaining hour is a bet. Spend one agonising over a button colour and you have none for the graph schema. The engineering skill is not "decide well" in the abstract — it is spending judgment in proportion to what reversal would cost. As implementation gets cheaper, "making and defending such decisions becomes more rather than less important." [SE Handbook] And rule 12 requires you to explain your technical decisions, so the decision and its reasoning have to exist somewhere you can point at.

Before committing, always answer the three questions. The answer decides how long you are allowed to think.

The idea

The handbook gives the rubric directly. Its Changeability decision asks: "How expensive will this decision be to reverse after we make it?" — because "the relevant quantity is … the cost of changing that decision after commitment." [SE Handbook] The consequence is a rule of thumb worth memorising:

"if it is expensive to change, try to get it right before committing; if it is cheap to change, do not spend more avoiding error than the error costs to correct." [SE Handbook]

And it names exactly where cost concentrates: "UI text, configuration, local algorithms, and isolated implementation details may be inexpensive to revise. Interfaces, persistent data representations, architectural boundaries, security protocols, external integrations, and certified behavior can become expensive or effectively irreversible." [SE Handbook] Map that onto Track 02 and the line falls cleanly:

DecisionClassHow to decide
Track / key-focus features / submission formatMandatoryNot a choice — build to spec. Do not reopen.
Graph schema (labels, relationship types, property keys), backend language, submission formatExpensive to reverseDecide deliberately. Draw it, weigh consequences, buy evidence if cheap.
Logging library, UI copy, local helper functions, which mock data to seedReversiblePick fast. Move on. Being wrong costs twenty minutes.

The handbook's architecture procedure supplies the second half — separate constraints from objectives, eliminate dominated options, "compare genuine tradeoffs", and "buy information when uncertainty could change the decision." [SE Handbook] The same discipline applies to autonomy: reversible exploration can proceed autonomously, but a hard-to-reverse action (the article's example is buying a flight) should drop back to a confirmation. "The level of autonomy is a design decision — and more autonomy is not always better." [BigData Boutique] The third question is the one that keeps you honest: what evidence would change my mind? "Do not spend effort obtaining information that cannot change the decision." [SE Handbook]

DECISION RECORD — three lines, keep it in the repo
DECISION: what you chose (one clause).
WHY:      the constraint/objective that decided it.
REVERSE:  cheap or expensive, and the seam that would contain a reversal.

Example:
DECISION: backend = Node/TypeScript
WHY:      one language across the Vite+React+TS app; FalkorDB has a first-class Node client
REVERSE:  expensive-ish, but contained behind the memory-service contract

Note what the AI is for here. It will happily hand you a confident verdict — and a verdict you cannot defend fails rule 12 and leaves you unable to debug the system you accepted. Ask it for the options and their consequences, plus a recommendation; then you pick, because you own the decision and must be able to explain it.

"Which should I use for the backend, Node or Python?"

"My backend must talk to FalkorDB and serve the existing Vite+React+TS cockpit; I know TS but not Python, and I have ~1 day. Lay out 2–3 backend options as a table: language, FalkorDB client maturity, what it costs me to learn, what it makes harder later. End with your recommendation and the single piece of evidence that would flip it. Do not decide for me — I will choose and write the decision record."

Which Track 02 decision is expensive-to-reverse and so deserves deliberate reasoning before you commit?

Run the rubric on your Node-vs-Python backend fork and write the decision record.

  1. Answer the three questions in one line each: Is it mandatory? (no). Reversible or expensive to reverse? What cheap evidence would change my mind? — e.g. "confirm the FalkorDB Node client can run my Cypher; if it can, TS wins on one-language cost."
  2. If the evidence is cheap, buy it: run one "hello graph" query from the Node client. Let the result decide, not the vibe. [SE Handbook]
  3. Write the three-line DECISION RECORD from above into docs/decisions.md in the repo. It becomes the answer to "why did you pick X?" during judging.
