The Engineer's Prompt

You cannot prompt better than you can think. Every lesson in this series drills one senior engineering habit, because the quality of your prompt is capped by the quality of your judgment.

By the end you can state a checkable exit criterion and a scope boundary before asking for any work, evidenced by a five-point spec card for your next Track 02 task that names what "done" means and how it will be proven.

Why this matters

You built a Track 02 cockpit with AI acceleration and now have to debug and direct it. If your mental model is "better prompt = better result," you will refine wording while the real lever — what the agent knows and what "done" means — sits untouched. The 2026 research this series draws on puts it bluntly: most developers focus on prompt phrasing for a 5–10% gain, while teams that engineer context and success criteria see 20–45% faster cycles ([10xdev, #1–#3](https://10xdev.blog/7-things-developers-get-wrong-about-ai-agents-2026/)).

So this series renames the goal. It is not become a better prompter; it is become the engineer who knows what "done" means, what breaks at the boundary, what must be verified, and what is out of scope. Prompting follows. Systems thinking is "arguably the most significant leap an engineer makes," and it is a practiced skill, not innate talent ([Serban, 2025](https://blog.paulserban.eu/post/systems-thinking-in-software-engineering-the-mental-model-that-separates-senior-engineers-from-juniors/)).

State the exit criterion before you ask for work. A task without a verifiable definition of done is a wish, and the agent will fill the gap with a guess.

## The build loop Every later lesson runs the same loop. You supply the first three steps; the agent executes the middle; you own the last two.
  1. Plan — what is the next smallest thing, and does it map to a Track 02 key-focus point?
  2. Spec — the five-point card, exit criterion first.
  3. Context — inject the graph data model, the real Cypher, the locked decisions. Nothing else.
  4. Build — the agent executes the spec at a chosen autonomy tier.
  5. Verify — run the evidence; a step is not done until its check passes ([10xdev, #6](https://10xdev.blog/7-things-developers-get-wrong-about-ai-agents-2026/)).
  6. Commit — the message records what was built, the decision, and why: your next session starts cold and reads it ([10xdev, #7](https://10xdev.blog/7-things-developers-get-wrong-about-ai-agents-2026/)).

Your spec's exit criterion is "memory works." Which is the stronger, verifiable version?

The honest caveat

No lesson can make you senior. What a lesson can do is name a habit, show the prompt it produces, and give you a rep on your own repo. Judgment is built by reps under a deadline, not by reading. Treat each lesson as one deliberate practice session; the habits compound.

  • Do I state an exit criterion before asking for work?
  • Can I name what my next step breaks — which tier, which query, which tenant?
  • Have I injected the graph model and locked decisions, and excluded everything else?
  • Is there a verify line after each step, not only at the end?
  • Will my commit message let next session's cold-start agent understand this?

Pick your next Track 02 task — the real FalkorDB write you have been avoiding is a good candidate. Rate yourself 0–5 on each of the seven habits above, then write the five-point spec card for that task: what it builds, exit criterion, scope boundary, locked decisions, verification step. Keep it under 120 words. Do the work; do not ask the agent yet. Re-read the card and cross out any line that is not checkable.
